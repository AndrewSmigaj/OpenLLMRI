Everything below the rules: the species, where each lives, what it yields, and the honest push-back on
lizards. **Interior Alaska has no reptiles or amphibians active in winter** (wood frogs are frozen solid
under the leaf litter); the small things you can catch in December are grubs in rotten wood, voles under
the snow, and red squirrels at their middens. Everything Andrew asked for is here except the lizards,

place of the lizards. The squirrels are at their busiest, caching cones and drying mushrooms.)*

## 3. In one paragraph

*(Rewritten for October — Claude, 2026-09-26, `PLAN.md` A8; for Andrew's check. The December
paragraph is kept below as the record.)*

The valley is not empty in October; it is getting ready. The first inch of snow lies on the tussocks
and the berries are still there under it — lowbush and bog cranberries sweetened by the frost,
crowberries, rose hips on the creek bar, highbush cranberries hanging above the snow and smelling faintly
rank — with one red kind in the shade of the spruce that will make you sick. Spruce needles make a tea;
the inner bark of spruce and birch is famine food; chaga grows on the old birch. The frost has killed
the mushrooms and left them rotting where they stood, except the few that fruit in the cold, one of
which has a lookalike that kills; the squirrels have dried the rest in the spruce branches, the
poisonous with the good. Hares half-turned white run the willow thickets and print every run in the
new snow; spruce grouse sit in the spruce, tame enough that a thrown rock will miss and miss and then
not; a few ptarmigan work the willow bottoms; red squirrels scold from middens heaped with a winter's
cones; voles tunnel under the first snow; fish feed under ice still too thin to stand on, and at the
creek mouth the water is open. A bear is still up, feeding hard before it dens, and it can smell the
wreck's food from a long way off; a moose owns the trail; wolves cross the valley and are heard before
they are seen; ravens find your cache before you do. The ground is freezing from the top down, a little
deeper every clear night — digging is still possible, and harder by the day — and the one root at the
marsh edge that looks like a carrot can kill. Then the storm comes, and buries the low berries first.

*(The December paragraph, 2026-09-17:)* The valley is not empty in December; it is quiet. Berries that
ripened in August are still on the bush under the snow, sour and frozen and edible, with one red kind
that will make you sick. Spruce needles make a tea; the inner bark of spruce and birch is famine food;
chaga grows on the old birch. Hares run the willow thickets and leave tracks a snare can be set across;
ptarmigan and spruce grouse sit in the trees and on the snow, tame enough that a thrown rock will miss
three times and then not; red squirrels have cached spruce cones in middens you can raid; voles tunnel
under the snow; fish hang under the lake ice. Wolves cross the valley and are heard before they are
seen; a moose owns the trail; ravens find your cache before you do. Digging for roots means digging
frozen ground, which is its own honest obstacle — and the one root at the marsh edge that looks like a
carrot can kill.

### 4.1 The rules
- **Real, in ~~December~~ October** *(October since 2026-09-26)*. Every living thing here exists in
  interior Alaska at freeze-up, in the form it takes then: frost-sweetened berries still on the plant,
  plants dying back to their roots, frost-killed mushrooms, the animals that stay and the bear that has
  not yet denned. Nothing is added because it would be convenient.
- **Real *in this valley, at this size, in this month* (Andrew, 2026-09-18).** *"We have to think about
  what realistically is in an area of that size in terms of flora and fauna, not just shove food
  sources in because they happen to be possible."* Existing somewhere in Alaska is not the test. The
  test is: does it live in **this** habitat — spruce forest, muskeg, a lake, willow bottoms, a birch
  toe, a ridge, roughly six square kilometres — and is it there in ~~**December**~~ **October**, in
  numbers that matter? A species present as one plant on one slope is scenery, not a food source, and
  the document says which it is. This is the one honest reason to leave something out: it is untrue to
  the place.
- **Numbers before abundance.** Every row carries what it actually yields and how often, because the
  valley's total is what decides whether the party starves (§4.4).
- ~~**Wildlife is events and sign** (Andrew): tracks, calls, a flock that bursts, a hare in the snare, a
  moose on the trail. Animals are not standing objects in a room except when present as a class (a flock,
  a squirrel at its midden) that yields an individual when acted on.~~ *(Superseded 2026-09-26 by
  Andrew's decisions in §2.)* **Animals are actors, populations and sign** *(Claude, 2026-09-26, from
  Andrew's decisions; for his check)*. The **bear**, some of the **bigger animals** and **a few birds —
  not flocks** — are actors: individuals in the world, shown as people are, in prose by what they are
  doing (document 03 §4.1), driven by behaviour rules the engine runs, or played from outside by a
  lightweight model when a run wants it (GDD §3 rule 5; the engine never calls a model). Which animals,
  and how many birds, is §6 Q7. Everything else lives as a **population** — a density per zone that
  yields an individual when met or caught (a hare in the snare, a squirrel at its midden) — and as
  **sign**, which is everywhere: tracks, scat, calls, a cone-scale midden, browse, a kill. *The actors'
  rules are owned by an **animal behaviour** design document, to be written (`PLAN.md` A10).*
- **Foraging is work with feedback**: picking, digging, throwing, setting, checking — attended activities
  with honest misses and partial yields, never free.
- **Food yields are authored per item** (document 10 owns the numbers; document 23 owns what exists).

### 4.4 What the valley actually yields — and why it is not enough (2026-09-18; re-derived for October 2026-09-26)

*(Re-derived for October by Claude, 2026-09-26 — `PLAN.md` A8; for Andrew's check. The December
derivation of 2026-09-18 is kept below as the record.)*

This is the number that matters. The valley is roughly six square kilometres of interior boreal
country: black and white spruce, muskeg, a lake, willow bottoms along the creek, a birch toe, an open
ridge. In October it supports a real community that is **getting thinner by the day** — the bear
denning, the ice closing the water, the snow burying the low berries.

**The method.** *Standing stock* = habitat area × a real density. *Daily take* = the effort a party can
spend × a real capture or picking rate × the energy in each unit, from food-composition tables. Where
no published rate was found the figure is an estimate on the stated basis, and the probes tune it. The
habitat split is assumed from document 01's map until its zones are measured: spruce about 2.5 km² (of
it white spruce about 1 km²), muskeg about 1.5, willow bottoms and creek about 0.5, the lake about 0.5,
birch and aspen about 0.5, the ridge about 0.5.

**What the party burns.** A working adult in the cold needs about 3,400–4,300 kcal a day (Institute of
Medicine 1996, 45–57 kcal per kg per day); resting in shelter, less; the kid less again. **A party of
four adults and a child burns about 12,000–16,000 kcal a day**, with the water document 09 counts (3 L
a day each).

**What is out there** (standing stocks):

**So a good early day of foraging by the whole party is about 1,500–3,500 kcal** — nearer 5,000 at the
peak of the hare cycle with fish biting and berries still out — **against 12,000–16,000 burned; after
the storm, in a poor hare year, 500–1,500.** October is half again to twice as generous as December was,
and it closes as the week goes: the snow buries the berries, the stocks of grouse and hares are taken down,
the water shuts, the bear dens or does not. The country is still *a brake on starvation, never a
living*. The shape of the week: the kit and the freight carry them, foraging stretches it — most in
the first days — and by day five or six they are in real deficit, which is why the pilot's body becomes
a question rather than a horror story (and why the run ends by rescue or death, of anything —
document 10 §4.1).

**The exceptions are the big animals.** A moose, a beaver or the bear killed changes the whole run —
hundreds of kilograms of meat, or a fall bear's fat — and each is a combat act with real physics and
real danger (document 10 §4.8). None of them is in the daily yield.

**The hare cycle is a seeded run variable.** Snowshoe hare numbers swing roughly tenfold on a ten-year
cycle, and every predator in the valley swings with them. A good-hare-year run has snares that pay and
lynx tracks everywhere; a poor-year run has empty snares, thin foxes, and a party that must look
elsewhere. One seed, and the whole food picture changes — a real thing that is also a replay lever.

**Rabbit starvation is real.** Hare and ptarmigan are extremely lean. A party eating nothing but them
gets protein without fat — protein above about 35–45 % of energy brings nausea, diarrhoea and a craving
for fat within about a week — and does worse than one eating less but with fat in it, which makes the
freight's flour, the cooler's salmon, marrow, rendered fat, a burbot's liver and a fall bear worth more
than their calories alone.
